Most technical hiring measures the wrong things: puzzle-solving under
observation, memorised algorithms, and the interviewer's mood.

### Skills-based assessment

Define the skills the role actually needs, at the level it needs them. Assess
each deliberately, with the same structure for every candidate, and score against
a rubric written in advance.

The gains are unglamorous and large: consistency between interviewers,
comparability between candidates, and defensibility of the decision. It also
dramatically reduces the bias that unstructured interviews reliably introduce.

What is worth assessing for senior engineers:

- **Design judgement** — trade-offs, not knowledge of patterns. Give them an
  ambiguous problem and watch what they ask.
- **Code quality** — real code, discussed. What would you change, and why.
- **Debugging** — a realistic broken system. Process matters more than speed.
- **Domain and business impact** — can they explain what their work changed for
  someone outside engineering.
- **Collaboration** — how they handle disagreement about a technical decision.
- **Working with AI** — increasingly the differentiator. Not whether they use
  it, but whether they can tell you where they stopped trusting it.

### Interviewers are a capability

An assessment framework applied inconsistently is not a framework. Train
interviewers deliberately — calibration sessions, shadowing, feedback on
write-ups — and treat interviewing as a skill people get better at rather than a
tax on senior engineers' time.

### Signals worth more than a CV

Tenure with depth beats a list of logos. Evidence of finishing something hard.
Open-source contributions, however small. And the ability to describe a failure
honestly — the strongest positive signal I know, and the one that separates
people who have shipped from people who have participated.
